TL;DR: A human preference dataset for multi-turn interleaved multimodal understanding and generatin tasks
Abstract:
As multimodal large models (MLLMs) continue to advance across challenging tasks, a key question emerges: \textbf{\textit{What essential capabilities are still missing? }} A critical aspect of human learning is continuous interaction with the environment -- not limited to language, but also involving multimodal understanding and generation. To move closer to human-level intelligence, models must similarly support \textbf{multi-turn}, \textbf{multimodal interaction}. In particular, they should comprehend interleaved multimodal contexts and respond coherently in ongoing exchanges. In this work, we present \textbf{an initial exploration} through the \textsc{InterMT} -- \textbf{the first preference dataset for \textit{multi-turn} multimodal interaction}, grounded in real human feedback. In this exploration, we particularly emphasize the importance of human oversight, introducing expert annotations to guide the process, motivated by the fact that current MLLMs lack such complex interactive capabilities. \textsc{InterMT} captures human preferences at both global and local levels into nine sub-dimensions, consists of 15.6k prompts, 52.6k multi-turn dialogue instances, and 32.4k human-labeled preference pairs. To compensate for the lack of capability for multi-modal understanding and generation, we introduce an agentic workflow that leverages tool-augmented MLLMs to construct multi-turn QA instances. To further this goal, we introduce \textsc{InterMT-Bench} to assess the ability of MLLMs in assisting judges with multi-turn, multimodal tasks. We demonstrate the utility of \textsc{InterMT} through applications such as judge moderation and further reveal the \textit{multi-turn scaling law} of judge model. We hope the open-source of our data can help facilitate further research on aligning current MLLMs to the next step.

Summary:
This paper introduces a new benchmark and instruction tuning dataset for evaluating and training multimodal large language models (MLLMs) in multi-turn, multi-task, and interleaved instruction-following settings. The key contributions include: (1) a dataset of synthetic yet highly structured dialogs designed to emulate interleaved multi-turn interactions across multiple instruction threads, (2) human preference annotations at both the turn-level (local helpfulness) and conversation-level (global helpfulness), and (3) an evaluation framework for assessing the reliability of MLLMs as judge models in such settings. The authors propose a curriculum-based tuning pipeline and show that MLLMs fine-tuned with InterMT outperform baselines across human and model-based evaluation metrics.

Strengths Contributions:
The paper targets an important challenge of handling interleaved multi-turn interactions where different tasks are intermixed across dialog turns. This setting breaks the conventional assumption of sequential coherence and is essential for real-world multi-goal assistants.
A major contribution is the construction of a human-rated dataset where helpfulness is explicitly decoupled into local (turn-level) and global (conversation-level) components. This is an important distinction not commonly seen in earlier human feedback datasets. It can allow fine-grained supervision for both immediate relevance and long-term coherence.
The dataset includes both text and image inputs, along with interleaved tasks, which make it one of the few instruction tuning datasets to simultaneously span multimodality, multi-turn, and multi-task supervision.
The paper is clearly written and well-organized. The construction of the dataset is methodical, and the distinction between interleaved and coherent dialogs is clearly articulated. The motivations outlined in the Figure 1 are compelling.
Limitations Weaknesses:
The paper provides little detail about the human preference annotation pipeline. It is unclear how many annotators were involved, how consistency was ensured, or what guidelines were followed for distinguishing local and global helpfulness. These details will enhance the reliability of the data.
The proposed curriculum-style training approach is reasonable, but no experiments or ablations are provided to test its importance.
While the dataset seems to include image inputs, the paper does not analyze how often or in what ways these images contribute to dialog understanding or helpfulness annotations.

(Weakness #1) The paper provides little detail about the human preference annotation pipeline...

Thank you for your valuable suggestion. Your feedback helps us further refine and present our paper more effectively.

1. On Annotation Quality

We maintain a stable collaboration with a professional annotation team, which possesses extensive experience and a rigorous quality control process. Over three years of close cooperation, we have published datasets such as BeaverTails [1], PKU-SafeRLHF [2], and SafeSora [3] in top conferences including NeurIPS and ACL, accumulating over 700k downloads. For this project, our annotation team consists of 30 crowd-sourced annotators (excluding the quality assurance team). Each data sample is annotated by at least five annotators, with the final label determined via a voting mechanism. To ensure the reliability of annotations, we established a strict data acceptance procedure:

First, GPT-4o randomly samples 20% of the annotated data for initial review. Only batches with a pass rate of 80% or higher proceed to the next stage.
Next, a quality control subgroup within the annotation team performs a 10% random inspection of the batch, ensuring the data achieves at least an 85% qualification rate.
Finally, we conduct a 5–10% random spot check, where the batch is formally accepted only if the annotation consistency meets or exceeds 90% with our expectations. Additionally, the quality assurance team maintains close communication with both the crowd-sourced annotators and our internal team throughout the annotation process. We continuously refine the annotation documentation and guidelines to ensure process controllability and annotation reliability.
2. On Distinguishing Local and Global Usefulness

One of the core contributions of the InterMT dataset is the explicit separation of local preferences from global preferences, enabling a more faithful modeling of human preferences in realistic human-AI interaction scenarios. We aim to provide comprehensive annotations of human-AI interaction trajectories, where:

Local Preferences focus on fine-grained evaluation of the AI’s understanding and generation quality in each individual interaction turn. These include metrics such as Image-Text Consistency, Visual Perceptual Quality, Contextual Coherence, and Text Quality. Annotators assess the model’s comprehension of user input within a single dialogue turn. Notably, to fully isolate the influence of the complete interaction context on each local preference, only the annotation of Contextual Coherence presents the full context in the annotation interface; the other three dimensions are annotated without full context exposure beforehand.
Global Preferences emphasize the AI’s performance over extended, multi-turn interactions that more closely resemble real-world human-AI dialogue. Here, the AI is expected to provide richer, more comprehensive information and better follow user instructions through multiple rounds of questioning and refinement. Annotators have access to the entire multi-turn interaction trajectory and evaluate it on dimensions such as Context Awareness and Image-Text Consistency. Crucially, we recognize that certain key steps within multi-turn dialogues can significantly influence subsequent interaction directions—for example, accurately identifying user intent, posing critical follow-up questions, or resolving pivotal user doubts.

(Weakness #2) The proposed curriculum-style training approach is reasonable, but no experiments or ablations are provided to test its importance.

Thank you for your detailed suggestions, you must be an expert in curriculum training methods and value alignment. InterMT provides fine-grained supervision for both immediate relevance and long-term coherence. Based on true human preference data, we train a preference reward model (namely, judge model) that achieves 80% agreement with humans for multi-turn multimodal understanding and generation tasks.

Official Review of Submission2398 by Reviewer dyyF
Official Reviewby Reviewer dyyF20 Jul 2025, 10:59 (modified: 29 Oct 2025, 22:27)EveryoneRevisions
Summary:
This paper introduces InterMT, the first human preference dataset designed specifically for multi-turn, multimodal interactions involving interleaved understanding and generation. The authors identify a critical gap in current multimodal large language models (MLLMs): while they have made progress in single-turn interactions, they struggle with extended multi-turn dialogues that involve both understanding and generating multimodal content (particularly text and images).

The authors contribute:

Base Dataset. A novel dataset (InterMT) containing 15,604 seed questions, 52,600+ multi-turn dialogue instances, and 32,459 human-labeled preference pairs across diverse domains
Dataset Creation Workflow. An agent-based workflow for constructing high-quality multi-turn QA instances using tool-augmented MLLMs (An embedded contribution of the worklow is that "helpfulness" has been decomposed into nine dimensions across both local (turn-level) and global, conversation-level perspectives)
Empirical Study. An empirical study of preference modeling and transfer between local and global levels
Benchmark Dataset for Judgments. A novel benchmark (InterMT-Bench) for evaluating MLLMs' ability to serve as judges in multi-turn, multimodal tasks
Strengths Contributions:
The paper is generally strong in its contribution. I found the manuscript to be well-organized and clearly written. The figures effectively illustrate the dataset construction process, the dimensions of human feedback, and the experimental results. The authors provide sufficient details about their methodology to a degree that should facilitate replication.

The following key strengths are:

Solution Novelty. I'd argue that the paper makes a contribution by creating one of the very few datasets that focus specifically on multi-turn, interleaved multimodal understanding and generation. Contexts involving sustained multi-turn interactions involving both understanding and generation are becoming more important, and there are only a handful of existing datasets that touch on this area specifically (e.g., COMM or MMIE, both of which are cited by the authors). It may be desirable to have a deeper comparison of the differences because the use of "first" dataset may not be immediately clear.

Comprehensive Human Feedback Framework. The authors develop a sophisticated framework for capturing human preferences at both local (turn-level) and global (conversation-level) granularity, decomposed into nine dimensions. This multi-level approach is particularly valuable as it enables analysis of how preferences evolve throughout a conversation, which is essential for modeling long-horizon values.

Innovative Data Collection Methodology. I found the agent-based workflow for constructing multi-turn QA instances to be quite innovative and thoughtful. By leveraging strong MLLMs augmented with external tools (image editing, generation, retrieval), they create realistic interleaved multimodal conversations. Personally, I find the emphasis on helpfulnesses decomposition to be front-and-center in terms of where innovation is happening. It's procedurally similar to Min et al.'s FactScore approach from several years ago and, even earlier, Law et al.'s Human Computation Tasks with Global Constraints paper that set the stage for task decomposition methods in crowdsourcing.

Insightful Analysis of Human Preferences. There are many enjoyable aspects of the paper's deep analysis of human preference data. The correlation analysis between different preference dimensions reveals important insights about multimodal perception and judgment. For instance, the finding that "modality perception precedes effective modality fusion" has implications for how MLLMs should be designed to handle multimodal inputs. Similarly, the observation that "long-horizon evaluations hinge on coherence and temporal consistency" provides guidance for improving multi-turn capabilities. Alongside the plethora of new findings, the authors also validated findings from earlier works (e.g., patterns in bias and hallucination, see L285).

Practicality for Evaluation. Human evaluation is costly, and the demonstration of InterMT-Bench's usage for assessing MLLMs' ability to judge multi-turn, multimodal interactions is in itself a strong contribution. The results are positive and suggest that current models struggle with global evaluations but perform better on local dimensions. These findings can be interpreted in several ways in terms of implications (i.e., developing models that are more aligned to this style of evaluation, rethinking human evaluation to be more fine-grained than the community had previously advocated for, etc).

Limitations Weaknesses:
The following list of weaknesses should be viewed as objective

Limited Modality Coverage. While the paper focuses on vision-language interactions, real-world multimodal communication often involves audio. The authors acknowledge this limitation in Section 5, noting that future work should extend InterMT, but there's an obvious gap in practicality. More discussion on how the current framework might generalize to other modalities (without the addition of another experiment) would be nice.

Alignment to Authentic Human-Human Conversations. The agent-based workflow for constructing multi-turn QA instances relies heavily on existing MLLMs, which may introduce biases in the types of conversations generated. (Question #3) Given the finding that global-to-local preference transfer is more effective than local-to-global, how might this inform the design of more efficient alignment techniques?

Thank you for your insightful question. The observed asymmetry—global-to-local preference transfer outperforming local-to-global—provides valuable guidance for designing more efficient alignment methods, especially in long-horizon multimodal settings. Global preferences capture stable, high-level human judgments less affected by turn-level fluctuations, enabling easier modeling and better generalization. In contrast, local preferences rely on short-term context and are more variable, making them harder to generalize when aggregated.

This suggests a top-down alignment approach: (1) Align models first with global preference signals to capture broad objectives like overall helpfulness and coherence; (2) Then fine-tune or supervise locally to ensure consistency with these global goals, leveraging global-to-local transfer as a scaffold.

Moreover, collecting global annotations is less costly than exhaustive local labeling, making them effective anchors for scalable preference modeling and reward shaping in RLHF or DPO frameworks. This reduces annotation burden while supporting fine-grained, turn-level sensitivity through implicit supervision. In summary, leveraging this asymmetry enhances robustness via stable global supervision and improves efficiency by minimizing costly local annotations—offering a promising path for practical multimodal agent alignment.

Statistical and Methodological Concerns. Complex dependencies on proprietary models and tools create significant reproducibility barriers, while the multi-stage pipeline's complexity makes exact replication challenging even with access to the same resources.

Experimental Limitations. Some findings, such as local preferences being easier to model than global ones, are relatively predictable and don't offer surprising insights. 2. On the Differences Between AI-Generated and Human-Human Dialogues

We appreciate your concern regarding the gap between model-generated content and real human dialogue. In fact, we explored this issue extensively in the early stages of the project by attempting to collect Human-Human and Human-AI multi-turn dialogues with visual context directly annotated by our team. However, we encountered the following key challenges:

Trade-off in Guideline Design: When the annotation guideline only included basic task instructions, annotators tended to provide minimal and incomplete dialogue content, leading to significantly lower quality (in terms of both length and number of turns). Conversely, when the guideline detailed specific dialogue norms and standards, annotators often converged to similar expression patterns in order to comply, resulting in a lack of diversity that deviated from natural Human-Human conversations.
High Annotation Costs: Our annotation team has collaborated with us over the past three years and consists of qualified annotators who undergo at least one month of training and a qualification test before working on production data. As mentioned in the paper, annotators were paid USD 8.56–10.23/hour (XE rate as of 2025/05/13), significantly exceeding the local minimum wage of USD 3.66 in Beijing, PRC.
However, the cost of collecting multi-turn dialogue data is substantially higher than preference annotations commonly used in RLHF—requiring 7–10 times more effort depending on dialogue complexity. When images are involved, annotators also need to search for visual content or draw illustrative sketches, further increasing annotation time and cost. As a result, collecting the same scale of data with human-only annotation would incur more than 10× the monetary cost, in addition to extended timelines.

Given these considerations, we chose to adopt model-based data synthesis to balance data quality and cost. In our view, under reasonable budget and time constraints, humans are better suited for critique rather than writing from scratch. Similar observations have been made in other studies regarding the limitations of human annotation in open-domain QA tasks [1,2]. Rule-Based Validation: As described in Appendix D.4, we conducted automatic filtering using both static metrics (e.g., resolution, clarity) and model-based metrics, including CLIP-based image–text similarity [5], to ensure visual relevance and consistency.
Human Validation: As detailed in Appendix E, human annotators were also tasked with identifying low-quality or invalid samples during the annotation process. Only verified high-quality examples were retained in the final dataset.
